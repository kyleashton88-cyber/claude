The DeFi Research Loop

1 · Mechanism

What does the protocol do at contract level? Identify users, assets, state changes and incentives.

2 · Cash Flow

Separate fees, borrower interest and real economic activity from token emissions or temporary incentives.

3 · Dependencies

Map oracles, bridges, admin keys, validators, stablecoins, governance, sequencers and third-party contracts.

4 · Solvency

Measure collateral quality, liquidation mechanics, liquidity depth, bad-debt pathways and concentration.

5 · Evidence

Verify claims using explorers, protocol docs, audits, governance forums and independent on-chain data.

6 · Exit

Define the exact unwind path, gas needs, slippage limits, approvals to revoke and emergency triggers.

HISTORY

How DeFi became a financial stack.

DeFi did not appear all at once. It evolved from digital cash, programmable blockchains, stablecoins, automated market makers, lending markets and increasingly composable infrastructure.

2009Bitcoin proves decentralised digital scarcity.

Public-key ownership, proof-of-work consensus and a transparent ledger establish the base idea: value can move without a traditional financial intermediary.

2014–2015Ethereum introduces general-purpose smart contracts.

The Ethereum whitepaper proposed a programmable blockchain; mainnet launched in 2015, making autonomous financial logic possible on a shared settlement layer.

2017MakerDAO and DAI push decentralised stable-value credit.

Collateral-backed stable assets demonstrate that lending, leverage and monetary primitives can be built from smart contracts.

2018–2020AMMs and money markets become core primitives.

Uniswap popularises automated liquidity pools while lending protocols make pooled, overcollateralised borrowing a standard DeFi building block.

2020“DeFi Summer” accelerates composability.

Liquidity mining, governance tokens and yield aggregation create rapid capital movement and expose the difference between sustainable fees and subsidised APY.

2021–2023Multichain expansion, bridges, L2s and liquid staking.

Capital spreads across chains and rollups; cross-chain risk, bridge exploits, oracle design and staking derivatives become central research topics.

2024–2026DeFi broadens into RWAs, institutional rails and autonomous agents.

Tokenised real-world assets, stablecoin payments, permissioned integrations and AI-driven on-chain execution expand the scope of decentralised finance.

CHAPTER 01

DeFi Foundations

What decentralised finance is, how protocols replace/intermediate traditional functions, composability, permissionless access and core risks.

DeFi stack

Blockchains, smart contracts, tokens, wallets, protocols, interfaces and data layers.

Self-custody

Users can control keys and sign transactions directly; key management becomes an operational responsibility.

Composability

Protocols can interact like building blocks, creating powerful integrations and dependency risk.

Permissionless systems

Public protocols may be accessible without a traditional intermediary, subject to protocol/interface and jurisdictional constraints.

Transparency

On-chain state can often be inspected publicly, but transparency does not remove smart-contract or economic risk.

Risk-first mindset

Yield is compensation for one or more risks; identify those risks before comparing headline returns.

CHAPTER 02

Blockchain & Transaction Mechanics

Blocks, transactions, confirmations, finality, gas, nonces, mempools, explorers and execution.

Transaction lifecycle

Create → sign → broadcast → mempool/queue → inclusion → confirmation/finality.

Gas / fees

Execution consumes network resources; fee models differ across chains.

Nonce

Account-based chains commonly use sequence values to order transactions and prevent replay.

Finality

Probabilistic or deterministic finality describes when reversal becomes unlikely or impossible under protocol assumptions.

Explorers

Inspect addresses, transactions, contracts, logs, token transfers and block data.

Failed transactions

A reverted transaction can still consume fees because computation/network resources were used.

CHAPTER 03

Wallets & Key Management

EOAs, smart wallets, seed phrases, private keys, hardware wallets, multisig, approvals and signing.

Private keys

The cryptographic authority used to sign actions; exposure can mean irreversible loss.

Seed phrase

Recovery secret capable of recreating wallet keys; never enter it into untrusted websites or messages.

Hardware wallet

Keeps signing keys in a dedicated device, reducing some online-key exposure.

Multisig

Requires multiple authorised signers or a defined threshold for execution.

Smart wallets

Contract-based accounts can support recovery, batching, policies and other programmable controls.

Signature hygiene

Read the domain, chain, token, spender, amount and action before signing.

Fungible tokens

Interchangeable units commonly represented by standards such as ERC-20 on EVM networks.

NFTs

CHAPTER 05

Stablecoins

Fiat-backed, crypto-collateralised, synthetic/algorithmic designs, peg mechanisms and depeg risk.

Fiat-backed

Issuer holds reserves and issues redeemable tokens subject to issuer/custodian terms.

Crypto-backed

On-chain collateral supports issued stable assets, often with overcollateralisation.

Synthetic designs

Use derivatives, hedging or protocol mechanisms to target a reference value.

Peg mechanics

Arbitrage, redemption, collateral and liquidity can help maintain a target price.

Depeg risk

Liquidity stress, reserve concerns, oracle failures or design breakdown can move price away from target.

Concentration

Using several stablecoins does not guarantee diversification if they share issuers, collateral or liquidity dependencies.

CHAPTER 06

Decentralised Exchanges

AMMs, order books, aggregators, routing, price impact, slippage and MEV-aware execution.

AMM

Automated market makers quote against liquidity pools using a pricing function.

On-chain order book

Orders are matched using price/time or protocol-specific rules on-chain or through hybrid systems.

Aggregator

Searches multiple venues/routes to improve execution under its routing model.

Price impact

Your trade changes the pool/market price; impact generally grows with order size relative to liquidity.

Slippage tolerance

Defines acceptable execution deviation; too tight can fail, too loose can expose poor fills.

Routing

Multi-hop swaps can improve price but add contracts, pools and execution complexity.

CHAPTER 07

AMM Mathematics

Constant product, concentrated liquidity, curves, spot price, pool ratios and fee mechanics.

Constant product

A common model maintains x·y=k before fees and liquidity changes.

Pool price

Reserve ratios imply the marginal exchange rate in simple constant-product pools.

Concentrated liquidity

LP capital can be allocated to selected price ranges instead of the entire curve.

Stable-swap curves

Specialised curves can reduce slippage around similarly priced assets.

Fees

Swap fees accrue according to protocol and position rules.

Arbitrage

External traders help realign pool prices with broader markets, creating LP inventory changes.

CHAPTER 08

Liquidity Providing

LP positions, fee income, inventory exposure, range selection, capital efficiency and rebalancing.

LP role

Provide assets to facilitate swaps and earn protocol-defined fees/incentives.

Inventory risk

Your asset mix changes as traders move price through the pool.

Range selection

Concentrated LPs choose active ranges; narrower ranges can increase capital efficiency and management burden.

Out-of-range

A concentrated position can become one-sided and stop earning swap fees until price returns, depending on protocol design.

Fee APR

Historical/estimated fee yield depends on volume, liquidity share, range and future activity.

Active management

Repositioning can add fees, taxable events and adverse timing; rules should be explicit.

CHAPTER 09

Impermanent Loss & LP P&L

Relative-value loss, fee offset, concentrated liquidity, divergence and benchmark comparison.

IL concept

LP value can underperform simply holding the deposited assets when relative prices move.

Benchmark

Compare LP outcome with the same starting assets held outside the pool.

Fee offset

Fees may offset some divergence loss but are not guaranteed to exceed it.

Concentration

Tighter ranges can increase both fee efficiency and sensitivity to price movement.

Realised outcome

Withdrawal crystallises the current LP inventory/value; 'impermanent' does not mean harmless.

Full P&L

Include token price changes, fees, incentives, gas, rebalancing and opportunity cost.

Lending Markets

Supply, borrow, utilisation, interest models, collateral factors, reserves and protocol liquidity.

Supply

Deposit assets into a lending market and receive protocol-defined yield/receipt position.

Borrow

Post eligible collateral and borrow supported assets within risk parameters.

Utilisation

Borrowed liquidity relative to supplied liquidity often influences variable rates.

Interest curve

Protocols may use kinked or other models to adjust rates as utilisation changes.

Collateral factor

Determines how much borrowing capacity an asset contributes.

Liquidity risk

High utilisation can make withdrawals/borrowing expensive or temporarily constrained.

CHAPTER 11

Collateral, LTV & Health Factor

Loan-to-value, liquidation thresholds, health metrics, collateral volatility and buffers.

LTV

Borrowed value divided by collateral value under the protocol's valuation rules.

Liquidation threshold

Risk boundary at which positions become eligible for liquidation.

Health factor

Protocol-specific metric summarising collateral safety; exact formulas differ.

Buffer

Operate materially away from liquidation rather than treating the threshold as a target.

Correlation

Borrowing one volatile asset against another can change risk depending on their relationship.

Monitoring

Collateral values, debt, rates and protocol parameters can change continuously.

CHAPTER 12

Liquidations

Liquidators, close factors, bonuses/penalties, oracle prices, cascading risk and defensive management.

Eligibility

When risk metrics breach protocol thresholds, some/all collateral may become liquidatable.

Liquidator

Third party/bot repays eligible debt and receives collateral under protocol rules.

Penalty/bonus

Liquidation incentives increase the cost of breaching risk thresholds.

Oracle dependency

Incorrect/delayed price inputs can affect liquidation behaviour.

Cascade

Falling collateral values and forced sales can reinforce market stress.

Defence

Lower debt, add collateral, hedge or close risk before thresholds are approached.

CHAPTER 13

Borrowing Strategies

Liquidity without selling, stablecoin borrowing, looping, leverage and debt management.

Liquidity borrow

Borrow against collateral to access capital without immediately selling the collateral.

Stablecoin debt

Reduces debt-asset volatility but collateral can still fall.

Looping

Borrow, redeposit and borrow again to amplify exposure/yield and liquidation sensitivity.

Leverage

Borrowed exposure magnifies gains, losses, fees and liquidation risk.

Rate risk

Variable borrow rates can rise sharply under utilisation stress.

Exit plan

Know how debt will be repaid if collateral falls, rates spike or liquidity disappears.

Inflows

Outflows

Realised value

Values units at a reference based on last movement/cost-basis methodology.

MVRV

Compares market value with realised-value framework; definitions vary by provider.

SOPR

Spent Output Profit Ratio estimates profit/loss of moved UTXO supply.

Dormancy

Weights movement by coin age to highlight older supply becoming active.

HODL waves

Age-band distribution of supply under UTXO-based methodology.

Limits

These metrics are strongest when definitions, chain model and historical behaviour are understood.
