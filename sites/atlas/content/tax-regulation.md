# Tax and regulation in Australia

This page is general information about how Australian tax and regulation apply to crypto assets, written for readers in Australia. It is not tax advice, legal advice, or financial advice, and it does not take your circumstances into account. Tax outcomes depend on facts this page cannot see, such as whether you are an investor or running a business, your residency, and how each transaction was structured. Rules and regulator guidance change. Check the linked official pages for the current position, and speak to a registered tax agent or a qualified adviser about your own situation.

Worked numbers below are invented. Each block is marked as a hypothetical example. Facts on this page were checked against the regulators' own websites on 10 November 2026.

## Who does what

Three Commonwealth bodies matter most for crypto in Australia. Each has a different job.

- **The Australian Taxation Office (ATO)** administers tax. It decides how crypto transactions are treated for income tax and capital gains tax, and it runs data-matching programs. Start at the ATO's crypto pages: [https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments).
- **The Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC)** regulates financial products and services, markets, and consumer protection for financial products. Some crypto assets and services are financial products and need licences. ASIC's consumer site, Moneysmart, has plain-English material on crypto: [https://moneysmart.gov.au/complex-investment-products/crypto-assets](https://moneysmart.gov.au/complex-investment-products/crypto-assets).
- **The Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre (AUSTRAC)** administers anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing law. Businesses that provide certain virtual asset services must register with it. The [exchanges page](exchanges.md#registration-in-australia-as-general-information) covers registration in more detail.

Scam reporting goes to Scamwatch and ReportCyber, listed on the [security page](security.md#where-to-report).

## Tax basics for crypto

### Crypto is usually a CGT asset

The ATO treats most crypto held as an investment as a capital gains tax (CGT) asset. Each crypto asset is a separate CGT asset. When you dispose of one, a CGT event happens, and you may make a capital gain or a capital loss.

The ATO lists these as disposals:

- selling crypto for Australian or foreign currency
- swapping or trading one crypto asset for another
- gifting crypto
- using crypto to buy goods or services.

A swap from one coin to another is a disposal even though no cash was received. This is the point people most often miss.

Moving crypto between wallets you own is not a disposal, as long as you keep ownership. If part of the amount is used to pay a network fee during the transfer, the ATO says that fee portion is a disposal.

See the ATO's page on transactions: [https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments/transactions-acquiring-and-disposing-of-crypto-assets](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments/transactions-acquiring-and-disposing-of-crypto-assets).

### Working out a gain or loss

A capital gain or loss is the difference between the **capital proceeds**, what you received or the market value of what you received, and the **cost base**, what it cost to acquire plus certain costs of holding and disposing of it. Both are measured in Australian dollars at the time of each transaction.

Capital losses reduce capital gains in the same financial year. A net capital loss cannot be deducted against other income such as wages. It can be carried forward to reduce capital gains in later years.

### The CGT discount

If you are an Australian resident for tax purposes and owned the asset for at least 12 months before the CGT event, you may be able to reduce the capital gain by 50 percent. Capital losses are applied before the discount. The days of acquisition and disposal are excluded when counting the 12 months. See the ATO's CGT discount page: [https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/capital-gains-tax/cgt-discount](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/capital-gains-tax/cgt-discount).

Hypothetical example. A resident individual buys a made-up coin for 2,000 Australian dollars, including fees. Eighteen months later they swap it for a different coin with a market value of 3,000 Australian dollars. The swap is a disposal. The capital gain is 3,000 - 2,000 = 1,000. They had no capital losses that year. Because they held the coin for more than 12 months, the discount may reduce the gain to 500, which is the figure that would be included in assessable income. The new coin's cost base starts at 3,000. Illustrative example on hypothetical data. Not a recommendation to trade this asset or these levels.

### Staking rewards and airdrops

The ATO generally treats staking rewards as ordinary income when you receive them, valued at their market value in Australian dollars at that time. That value also becomes the cost base of the reward tokens, so a later disposal of them is a separate CGT event. Airdrops are generally treated as ordinary income too. See [https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments/transactions-acquiring-and-disposing-of-crypto-assets/staking-rewards-and-airdrops](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments/transactions-acquiring-and-disposing-of-crypto-assets/staking-rewards-and-airdrops).

### DeFi and wrapping

The ATO has published guidance on decentralised finance. It says many DeFi "lending" and "borrowing" arrangements, and deposits into liquidity pools, can trigger a CGT event because beneficial ownership changes. Its current page also says that wrapping a token through a smart contract is a CGT event, referencing a draft determination. Draft guidance can change before it is final. Read the current page before relying on it: [https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments/decentralised-finance-and-wrapping-crypto](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments/decentralised-finance-and-wrapping-crypto).

### Personal use assets

A crypto asset can be a personal use asset if it is kept or used mainly to buy items for personal use or consumption. A capital gain on a personal use crypto asset acquired for less than 10,000 Australian dollars is exempt. The ATO's view is that this exemption is narrow. Crypto held as an investment, even if some of it is later spent, is generally not a personal use asset. Capital losses on personal use assets are disregarded. See [https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments/crypto-asset-as-a-personal-use-asset](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments/crypto-asset-as-a-personal-use-asset).

### Lost or stolen crypto

If crypto is lost or stolen and you cannot recover it, you may be able to claim a capital loss if you can show evidence of ownership and of the loss. A lost private key is an example the ATO gives. Being the victim of a scam is a matter to report as well as to record. See [https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments/transactions-acquiring-and-disposing-of-crypto-assets/loss-or-theft-of-crypto-assets](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments/transactions-acquiring-and-disposing-of-crypto-assets/loss-or-theft-of-crypto-assets).

### Investor or trader

Most individuals hold crypto as investors, and the CGT rules above apply. Someone carrying on a business of trading crypto may instead be taxed on trading profits as ordinary income, with different rules for losses and stock. Whether activity is a business depends on facts such as scale, regularity, and organisation. This is a question for a registered tax agent, not for a web page.

## Record keeping

The ATO expects you to keep your own records. Relying on a venue to keep them is not enough, and venues can close or lose data. For each transaction, the ATO lists records such as:

- the date and time
- what the transaction was for, and the other party, which can be a wallet address
- the value in Australian dollars at the time
- receipts for buying, transferring, or disposing
- venue records and digital wallet records
- agent, accountant, and legal costs, and software costs for managing your tax affairs.

Keep records for five years from the later of when you prepared or obtained them, when the transaction was complete, or the year the CGT event happened. The ATO also suggests exporting your transaction history at least every three months. See [https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments/keeping-crypto-records](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/crypto-asset-investments/keeping-crypto-records).

The ATO runs a crypto data-matching program that compares what people report with transaction data from service providers. Accurate records are the simplest way to make your return match.

A trading [journal](journal-lab.md) is not a tax record by itself, but the same habits help. Record every fill, every fee, and every transfer between wallets, with a date and an Australian-dollar value.

## Regulation basics

### AUSTRAC and virtual asset service providers

Reforms to Australia's anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing law took effect on 31 March 2026. AUSTRAC now uses the term virtual asset service provider (VASP). Businesses that were registered as digital currency exchange providers became VASPs automatically from that date. AUSTRAC says it is illegal to provide virtual asset services in Australia without being registered.

Registration is a legal status. It is not a safety rating and not an endorsement. You can search AUSTRAC's public register at [https://online.apps.austrac.gov.au/vaspr](https://online.apps.austrac.gov.au/vaspr). AUSTRAC's overview is at [https://www.austrac.gov.au/industry-and-business/your-industry/virtual-asset-service-providers/virtual-asset-service-providers-overview](https://www.austrac.gov.au/industry-and-business/your-industry/virtual-asset-service-providers/virtual-asset-service-providers-overview).

Identity checks when you open an account with a registered business are part of these obligations.

### ASIC and financial products

Under current law, some crypto assets and related services are financial products. Examples ASIC discusses include certain staking services, some stablecoins, wrapped tokens, and derivatives such as futures on crypto. Businesses offering financial products or services generally need an Australian financial services licence. ASIC's Information Sheet 225 sets out its view: [https://asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/digital-transformation/digital-assets-financial-products-and-services/](https://asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/digital-transformation/digital-assets-financial-products-and-services/).

ASIC also notes that consumers are only protected by the laws it administers to the extent that a product is covered by them. If something is unlicensed or unregulated in Australia, it is harder to get help when things go wrong.

### New digital asset platform laws

The Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Act 2026 passed Parliament on 1 April 2026 and received Royal Assent on 8 April 2026. It brings digital asset platforms and tokenised custody platforms into the financial services licensing regime, administered by ASIC. ASIC is consulting on standards and guidance during 2026. The Act's commencement is in April 2027. [VERIFY: ASIC pages give commencement as 9 April 2027, while the Act's commencement table reads 8 April 2027. Confirm the date before publishing.] ASIC's roadmap is at [https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/news-items/asics-roadmap-for-digital-assets-law-reform-implementation](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/news-items/asics-roadmap-for-digital-assets-law-reform-implementation).

What this means in practice for a reader is simple to state and slow to arrive: the rules for the platforms you might use are changing, and the licence status of a business is something to check, not assume.

## Getting help

- **A registered tax agent.** Only registered tax agents can charge a fee to prepare your return or give tax agent services. The Tax Practitioners Board keeps a public register at [https://www.tpb.gov.au/](https://www.tpb.gov.au/).
- **The ATO.** Its crypto pages and calculators cover the common cases.
- **Moneysmart.** ASIC's consumer information on crypto risks and scams.
- **Scamwatch and ReportCyber.** If money has been taken. See the [security page](security.md#where-to-report).

## Common mistakes

- Treating a coin-to-coin swap as tax-free because no cash came out.
- Relying on a venue's export alone, then losing access when the venue changes or closes.
- Forgetting staking rewards as income.
- Assuming a business is safe because it appears on a register.
- Assuming that a foreign venue is outside Australian rules for Australian residents.
