# Macro and ETF flows

Crypto assets trade inside a wider financial system. Interest rates, the US dollar, the amount of money moving through markets, and scheduled economic releases all change the backdrop that charts sit in. This page explains those forces and how fund flows into exchange-traded funds are reported. It is general information. It does not forecast rates, prices, or flows, and the relationships described here have broken down before and can again. It is not personal advice. Consider your own circumstances.

Worked numbers below are invented. Each block is marked as a hypothetical example. Short definitions are in the [glossary](glossary.md#macro).

## Why macro matters to a chart

A chart shows what traded. It does not show why large holders were adding or reducing risk at that moment. Macro is one part of that why. When borrowing is cheap and money is plentiful, investors tend to accept more risk across many assets at once. When borrowing is expensive or money is scarce, the reverse tends to happen. Markets move between [risk-on](glossary.md#risk-on) and [risk-off](glossary.md#risk-off) states, and crypto has often moved with other risk assets during those shifts.

"Often" is the key word. The [correlation](glossary.md#correlation) between crypto and other markets has changed over time, and a relationship measured over one period is not a law. Treat every macro link on this page as context to check, not a rule to trade.

## Interest rates

A central bank sets a short-term policy rate. In Australia that is the cash rate, set by the Reserve Bank of Australia's Monetary Policy Board, which meets eight times a year. In the United States it is the federal funds target range, set by the Federal Open Market Committee, which also has eight scheduled meetings a year. Schedules are published in advance on each bank's site: [https://www.rba.gov.au/schedules-events/board-meeting-schedules.html](https://www.rba.gov.au/schedules-events/board-meeting-schedules.html) and [https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm).

Higher policy rates raise the return on cash and short-term government debt. That raises the bar for riskier assets, which must offer more to attract money. Lower rates do the opposite. Markets usually price expected rate moves before the meeting, so the reaction on the day often depends on the gap between the decision and what was expected, not on the decision alone.

The [yield curve](glossary.md#yield-curve) plots government bond yields across maturities. When longer yields sit below shorter ones, the curve is inverted. That shape has often been read as a sign of expected slowing. It is a description of current yields, and its record as a timing tool is mixed.

See [interest rate](glossary.md#interest-rate) for the one-line definition.

## The US dollar

Most crypto trading is priced against the US dollar or dollar-linked stablecoins. The [DXY](glossary.md#dxy) is a widely quoted index of the dollar against a basket of six major currencies: the euro, yen, pound, Canadian dollar, Swedish krona, and Swiss franc. A stronger dollar tightens financial conditions for borrowers outside the US and has at times coincided with weaker risk assets. A weaker dollar has at times coincided with the reverse.

For Australian readers there is a second layer. A crypto asset priced in US dollars can move differently in Australian dollars, because the exchange rate moves too. A flat US-dollar chart can be a rising or falling Australian-dollar chart. Record which currency your journal uses.

## Liquidity

In macro, liquidity means the overall availability of money and credit in the financial system, not the depth of one order book. Central bank balance sheets, government spending and borrowing, and bank lending all feed into it. Analysts build "global liquidity" measures from these pieces, and different measures disagree.

The broad idea is that more money looking for a home tends to support asset prices across the board, and less money tends to weigh on them. The timing is loose, the measures are revised, and the link is clearer in hindsight than in real time. Treat any single liquidity chart as one input.

## Inflation and the event calendar

[CPI](glossary.md#cpi), the consumer price index, measures changes in the prices households pay. Central banks watch inflation closely when setting rates. A CPI release that surprises in either direction can change rate expectations within minutes.

Scheduled releases that often move markets include:

- Central bank rate decisions and the press conferences or statements that follow.
- CPI in the US, released monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and in Australia, released monthly by the Australian Bureau of Statistics. Since November 2025 the ABS complete Monthly CPI has been Australia's headline inflation measure, replacing the quarterly figure in that role.
- Employment reports.
- Minutes of earlier central bank meetings.

Around these events, spreads can widen, liquidity can thin, and price can move sharply in both directions. Stops can fill worse than planned. Many traders reduce size or stand aside around scheduled releases. Whatever your rule, write it in the plan before the week starts, and note the event in the [journal](journal-lab.md) so you can see later how your trades behaved around releases.

## ETFs and how flows are reported

An [ETF](glossary.md#etf), exchange-traded fund, is a fund whose units trade on a securities market like shares. A spot crypto ETF holds the underlying coin, usually with a custodian, and its unit price tracks the coin's price minus fees.

In the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission approved the listing of spot bitcoin exchange-traded products on 10 January 2024, and they began trading the next day. Spot ether products began trading on 23 July 2024. The SEC chair's statement at the time said approval was not an endorsement of bitcoin. Other countries, including Australia, also have listed crypto funds. Atlas does not name or compare products.

**Creations and redemptions.** ETF units are created and redeemed in large blocks by authorised participants. When demand for units exceeds supply, new units are created and the fund acquires more of the underlying coin. When units are redeemed, the fund releases coin. These are the [ETF flows](glossary.md#etf-flow) you see reported.

**How flows are reported.** Issuers publish holdings, usually daily. Data providers compile net flows across funds from those disclosures. Figures can arrive after the trading day, can be revised, and differ between providers depending on which funds and which cut-off times they use.

**How to read them.**

- A net inflow means units were created on that day, in aggregate across the funds counted.
- A net outflow means units were redeemed.
- A flow describes the past day. It does not say what the next day will bring.
- Large flows can coincide with large price moves, and the direction of cause is often unclear. Price can drive flows as easily as flows drive price.

Hypothetical example. A made-up set of three funds reports, for one day, creations worth 120 million, 30 million, and redemptions of 90 million, in a common currency. Net flow for the set is 120 + 30 - 90 = 60 million. A second data provider that excludes the third fund reports 150 million. Both figures are correct for what they include. Illustrative example on hypothetical data. Not a recommendation to trade this asset or these levels.

## The halving cycle

Bitcoin's block subsidy halves every 210,000 blocks, roughly every four years. The [halving](glossary.md#halving) cuts the rate of new supply. Past halvings were followed, at various delays, by large rallies and then deep declines, and many people describe a four-year cycle.

There have been only a handful of halvings. That is a very small sample. Each one happened in a different macro environment, with different market participants, and in recent years with ETF demand that did not exist before. A pattern drawn from four data points is a story to test, not a schedule. The [crypto fundamentals](crypto-fundamentals.md) page explains the supply mechanics.

## Correlations

A [correlation](glossary.md#correlation) measures how closely two series moved together over a window. It ranges from minus one, perfectly opposite, to plus one, perfectly together. It describes the window you measured.

Correlations that have been widely discussed include crypto against technology shares, against gold, and against the dollar. Each has changed sign or strength over different periods. A [correlation regime](glossary.md#correlation-regime) can shift quickly, often in stress, when many assets fall together.

Hypothetical example. Over a made-up 90-day window, the correlation between a crypto asset and a share index is 0.6. Over the following 90 days it is 0.1. Neither figure says what the next 90 days will show. A position sized on the assumption of 0.6 would carry a different risk if the true figure had changed. Illustrative example on hypothetical data. Not a recommendation to trade this asset or these levels.

## Using macro without forecasting

A practical way to use this page:

1. Keep a calendar of scheduled events for the week.
2. Note the current direction of policy rates and the dollar in one line each.
3. Mark whether markets broadly look risk-on or risk-off, and why you think so.
4. Size and plan around event risk, and record what you did.
5. Review in the journal whether macro context changed your results, over a large sample.

None of those steps tells you the next move. They make sure you know what is on the calendar and what the backdrop looks like when you take a trade, and that your notes say so.
