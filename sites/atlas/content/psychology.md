# Trading psychology

Most trading mistakes are not about missing information. They are about acting against a plan you already had. This page describes the common biases, what they look like in the moment, and the routines people use to catch them. It is general information. It is not a mental health service, it is not personal advice, and nothing here makes losses less likely in a way anyone can promise. If trading is affecting your sleep, your relationships, or your finances in ways that worry you, step away and talk to someone you trust or a qualified professional.

Worked numbers below are invented. Each block is marked as a hypothetical example. Definitions are in the [glossary](glossary.md#fomo). The record-keeping that makes these patterns visible is on the [journal page](journal-lab.md).

## Why a plan is not enough

A plan is written when you are calm. Trades happen when you are not. Price moves quickly, money is visibly changing, and other people are posting their results. In that state the brain reaches for shortcuts. Those shortcuts are useful in daily life and costly on a chart.

The goal is not to remove emotion. That is not possible. The goal is to notice it early and have a rule already written for what you do next.

## The common biases

### Fear of missing out

[FOMO](glossary.md#fomo) is the urge to enter because price is moving and you are not in it. It usually shows up as an entry with no level, a stop placed wherever feels tolerable, and a size larger than normal to make up for being late.

**What it looks like.** Opening the chart because of a social post. Entering after a large candle with no pullback. Telling yourself the plan does not apply this time.

**A routine against it.** A rule that you only enter at a level written before the move started. If the move left without you, it left. Write the missed trade in the journal as a missed trade, which is a neutral record, not a loss.

### Revenge trading

[Revenge trading](glossary.md#revenge-trading) is taking a new trade quickly after a loss to win the money back. The trade is about the last loss, not about the current chart.

**What it looks like.** A second trade within minutes of a stop. A larger size than the first. A different setup you would not normally take.

**A routine against it.** A cooling-off rule: after a stop, no new entry for a set time, or until a full review of the losing trade is written. Some traders set a daily loss limit, in R, after which they close the platform for the day.

### Loss aversion

[Loss aversion](glossary.md#loss-aversion) is feeling a loss more strongly than a gain of the same size. On a chart it leads to two opposite mistakes: cutting winners early to lock something in, and holding losers past the stop in the hope they come back.

**What it looks like.** Moving a stop further away once price approaches it. Closing a winner at the first pullback even though the plan had a target.

**A routine against it.** Hard stops placed at entry, and a rule that a stop may move only in the direction that reduces risk. Measuring results in [R](risk-academy.md#r-multiples) also helps, because a loss of 1 R reads as the plan working, not as a personal failure.

### Overconfidence

Overconfidence is believing your read is more reliable than your record shows. It often follows a run of wins, which may be ordinary variance.

**What it looks like.** Raising size after a good week. Skipping the checklist. Taking setups outside the written framework because you "can see it".

**A routine against it.** Size changes only on a schedule, after a review of a sample large enough to mean something. A good week is not a sample.

Hypothetical example. A trader with a made-up record of ten trades has seven winners. Every winner is +1 R and every loser is -1 R at the original size, so the first ten return +4 R. The trader then doubles size. The next ten include three winners and seven losers at the doubled size, which is 2 * (3 - 7) = -8 R. After twenty trades the account is at -4 R, even though the twenty together are an even split of ten winners and ten losers. The size change was based on ten trades, which is too few to separate skill from variance. Illustrative example on hypothetical data. Not a recommendation to trade this asset or these levels.

### Confirmation bias

[Confirmation bias](glossary.md#confirmation-bias) is noticing evidence that agrees with a view you already hold and discounting evidence that does not.

**What it looks like.** Searching for posts that agree with your position. Switching timeframe until the chart looks like your thesis. Ignoring the indicator that disagrees.

**A routine against it.** Before entry, write one sentence on what would make the idea wrong. During the trade, check that sentence, not the feed.

### Anchoring

Anchoring is fixing on a past price, such as where you bought or a previous high, and judging everything against it. The market does not know your entry price.

**What it looks like.** Refusing to close because price is "below where I bought". Treating an old all-time high as a target with no other reason.

**A routine against it.** Review open positions as if you had no position: would you enter here, with this stop, today? If not, the reason to stay in needs to be in writing.

### Recency bias

Recency bias is giving the last few candles or trades more weight than the longer record. A few losses make a sound framework feel broken. A few wins make a weak one feel strong.

**A routine against it.** Judge frameworks on a set sample size, decided in advance, and review on a schedule rather than after each trade.

## Tilt

Tilt is a state, not a single bias. It is the point where several of the above combine and decisions stop following the plan. Signs include faster clicking, larger size, shorter timeframes, and a feeling that you must act now.

A tilt rule is simple and written in advance:

1. Name the signs that apply to you. Keep the list short.
2. When one appears, stop opening new positions.
3. Manage open positions only by the rules already set: existing stops and targets.
4. Leave the screen for a set time.
5. Write down what happened before you return.

The rule only works if it is decided while calm. Inventing it during tilt does not work.

## Routines

### Before the session

- Check what is already open and its total risk, the [portfolio heat](glossary.md#portfolio-heat).
- Note any scheduled events, such as central bank decisions, on the [macro page](macro-lab.md).
- Write the setups you are watching and the levels at which each would be valid.
- Decide the daily loss limit in R.

### Before each trade

This is the same short check as the [risk academy](risk-academy.md#a-pre-trade-check), with one psychological line added:

1. Cash at risk if the stop fills, including slippage.
2. Stop distance and the size it implies.
3. Portfolio heat after this trade.
4. The invalidation, written so someone else could check it.
5. Why this is not the same bet as positions already open.
6. **How you feel right now, in one word.** If the word is "behind", "angry", "bored", or "certain", wait.

### After the session

- Record every trade in the journal, including missed and skipped ones.
- Tag any trade that broke a rule with the rule it broke.
- Write one sentence on what you will do differently, or nothing if the plan was followed.

## Separating process from outcome

A trade can follow the plan and lose. A trade can break the plan and win. If you only judge by the result, you will reward rule-breaking whenever it happens to pay. Over time that trains the wrong habits.

A useful split in the journal is two scores for each trade: one for whether the plan was followed, and one for the result in R. Review the first score more often than the second. Over a large sample, the process score is the part you control.

## When to stop

Trading is optional. If you notice that you are trading to recover losses you cannot afford, borrowing to trade, hiding activity from people close to you, or unable to step away, those are signs to stop and seek support. In Australia, the National Debt Helpline offers free, confidential financial counselling on 1800 007 007 and at [https://ndh.org.au/](https://ndh.org.au/). Your doctor can help with stress or anxiety.
