# Video scripts: On-Chain Operator Program

Generated by `export/build_video.js` from `export/videos.js` (edit the scripts there, then rebuild).

**Voice:** Kokoro TTS, voice `af_heart`, synthesised sentence by sentence with measured pauses, then mastered
(high-pass, de-ess, compression, presence EQ, −16 LUFS). Marketing videos carry an original ambient music bed
that ducks under the voice. To use a human voice, record the VO column line by line and replace the audio track,
or re-run with `VOICE=am_michael` etc.
**Motion:** captions follow the voice word by word; bullets and steps reveal and highlight as they're spoken;
living node-network background, progress bar and chapter tag. Every video also gets `thumbs/<id>.jpg`,
`captions/<id>.vtt` and `chapters/<id>.txt`.

Compliance: no income or return claims, no fake urgency, keys never requested, disclaimer on screen.

| 7 | 1:28–1:46 | One village. / One notebook. — The notebook of who paid whom is called a ledger. | Picture a village with one notebook. Every time someone pays someone else, the village clerk writes it down. Ana paid Ben 10 coins. Ben paid Cai 5. Everyone trusts the notebook, because everyone trusts the clerk. That notebook has a name. It's called a ledger. |
| 8 | 1:46–2:10 | The clerk is your bank: `assets/diagrams/story-bank-vs-you.png` + zoom | Your bank is that clerk. It keeps the record for you. It can reverse a payment if something goes wrong. It can help when you forget a password. But it can also freeze your account, and you have to trust it completely. Crypto changes who's in charge. In your own wallet, the network keeps the record, no one can reverse a payment, no one can reset your password, and no one can freeze it but you. |
| 9 | 2:10–2:34 | From Lesson 0.1: `assets/diagrams/ledgers-vs-blockchain.png` + zoom | So what if the village didn't need a clerk at all? What if, instead of one notebook, every villager kept an identical copy, and they all checked each new entry together? That's the idea behind a blockchain. It's a shared ledger. Thousands of computers keep the same copy, and old entries can't be quietly changed. The trade-off is simple, and it matters: there's no undo. |
| 10 | 2:34–2:59 | Thousands of copies: `assets/diagrams/story-many-copies.png` + callouts | Here's why nobody can cheat. Imagine someone edits their copy to say they received 100 coins. Every other copy disagrees, so the network simply ignores the edited one. To fake a payment, you'd have to overpower most of the network at once, which is enormously expensive. No single company is in charge, and that's also why no one can undo a payment for you. |
| 11 | 2:59–3:23 | Why it's called a chain: `assets/diagrams/story-chain-of-blocks.png` + zoom | And why the word chain? New entries are grouped into pages, called blocks. Each block carries a kind of seal, a fingerprint made from everything written on it, plus the seal of the page before. So the pages are linked. Change one old entry, and its seal changes, and every page after it stops matching. That's why history on a blockchain can't be quietly rewritten. |

| 28 | 8:51–8:55 | Title card: 4 Roads and stamps | Chapter 4. Roads and stamps. |
| 29 | 8:55–9:28 | Networks are roads: `assets/diagrams/story-networks-roads.png` + callouts | Now Sam wanted to move crypto from the exchange into their own wallet. That's when they met networks. Think of networks as separate roads. Ethereum is the original road: the most secure, but with higher fees. Layer 2s are faster, cheaper roads built on top of Ethereum, and there are several of them. Your address can exist on many roads at once, but a parcel only travels on the road it was sent on. Send on a road your wallet doesn't support, and the money can be very hard, or impossible, to get back. |
| 30 | 9:28–9:56 | Gas is a stamp: `assets/diagrams/story-gas-stamp.png` | Every trip on those roads needs a stamp. It's called gas. When you send a transaction, you pay a small fee in the network's own coin, which on Ethereum and most layer 2s is ETH. The computers that run the network, called validators, check your transaction and add it to a block. When roads are busy, stamps cost more. And one detail catches people out: if a transaction fails, the stamp is usually still spent. |
| 31 | 9:56–10:12 | Flow: Sign → Network → Block → Arrives | Here's what happened when Sam pressed send. Sam's wallet signed the transaction with their private key.
